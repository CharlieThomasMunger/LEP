<h2>Separate the curriculum from the school</h2>

<p>An English-medium education in Luxembourg does not restrict a student to a Luxembourg university. The UK, US, Netherlands, Switzerland, Canada and Luxembourg itself can all be considered, depending on the diploma, subjects, grades and languages required.</p>

<p>A Levels are not confined to UK applications, and the IB is not useful only for the US. A country label is an inadequate basis for choosing the last two years of school.</p>

<p>Here, “IB” means the two-year Diploma Programme, normally taken within the 16–19 age range. Lower-secondary programmes are a separate question. IGCSEs usually precede A Levels and should not be confused with them.</p>

<h2>How the daily workload differs</h2>

<p><strong>The IB asks students to manage several strands together.</strong> It normally combines six subjects, three or four at Higher Level, with the extended essay, Theory of Knowledge, and Creativity, Activity, Service. Reading, writing, research and time management run through the programme.</p>

<p>It is worth considering for a balanced student who enjoys discussion and research and can manage several deadlines. Breadth can also be useful while a student’s interests are still developing.</p>

<p><strong>A Levels allow earlier concentration.</strong> Students commonly study three or four subjects in depth. This can suit a pupil with clear strengths who wants to develop them intensively.</p>

<p>Fewer subjects do not mean choosing only the easiest combination. Engineering, economics and medicine can require different mathematics and science preparation. Some universities or countries also require a broader prior subject record.</p>

<p>My starting point is how the child learns and which route can develop their strengths. Claims that one diploma is universally “better” or “easier” do not settle that question.</p>

<h2>My observations of the schools also affect the recommendation</h2>

<p>In my experience, <strong>ISL leans more towards an American-style emphasis on freedom and self-directed development; it does not press academic results as tightly as St George’s</strong>. That may not match a family expecting close, traditional supervision of marks and progress.</p>

<p>St George’s has struck me as rigorous and professional, with clear academic expectations. OTR feels smaller and more personal; space is limited, but contact with teachers is close.</p>

<p>These are my judgements from local visits and discussions. I consider the child in the actual school environment, alongside the course structure.</p>

<p>These are directions to investigate, not a promise that every subject combination opens every degree. Universities examine what the student actually studied and achieved.</p>

<h2>Switzerland requires language and academic planning together</h2>

<p>French-speaking and German-speaking university routes need more than the corresponding language. ETH Zurich’s bachelor’s programmes are mainly taught in German, and its academic admission conditions must also be met. EPFL expects French preparation alongside specific diploma and subject requirements, including mathematics and physics.</p>

<p>A student considering Swiss engineering should not avoid the necessary mathematics or physics and expect language study alone to resolve the problem later. UK engineering and Dutch economics also make early mathematics planning important.</p>

<p>When a degree is still undecided, I prefer to keep useful subject options open rather than close doors simply to make current marks easier to obtain.</p>
