<p class="lead">ISL’s facilities, activities and international profile are considerable strengths. My first question is whether the child will thrive in its independent learning culture and whether the family can provide the involvement the school expects.</p>
<figure class="article-photo"><img alt="James Liu visiting ISL. LEP photograph." loading="eager" src="/assets/fieldwork/isl.jpg"/><figcaption>James Liu visiting ISL. LEP photograph.</figcaption></figure><h2>Understand the curriculum by age</h2><p>ISL covers early childhood through secondary school. It is authorised for the IB Primary Years Programme and Diploma Programme; its Middle Years Programme is currently at candidate stage. Younger pupils encounter inquiry-led learning, while older pupils need to consider the Diploma and subject choices in relation to university plans.</p><h2>My observation: independence matters</h2><p>Of ISL, St George’s and OTR, I consider ISL’s facilities the strongest. Activities and interests are broad. Its educational atmosphere feels more American and exploratory, whereas St George’s, in my experience, follows academic progress and grades more tightly. A child who benefits from strong external structure may experience that difference very differently from a self-directed learner.</p><p>This distinction matters to families used to close academic supervision. Excellent resources only help when a child can use them: participation, friendships, confidence and independent work all matter.</p><h2>A school with an established local international community</h2><p>Among families I encounter, ISL attracts international-company employees and executives, affluent international households and some officials’ families. Employer-funded fees are part of this picture. Its established history, recognisable name and IB route contribute to its prominence. Many European international families I meet favour IB, while familiarity with British education often draws families towards A Levels.</p><h2>When parents live abroad</h2><p>ISL places substantial importance on parental participation. Our discussions with admissions staff and senior leadership have been cautious about children moving without their parents. Host-family arrangements receive close scrutiny. In applications I have encountered, there were two assessment sessions of about two hours; fewer than half of the online-assessment candidates in cases known to me passed. This is my case experience, not an official school-wide statistic.</p><h2>Chinese pupils: questions I ask in person</h2><p>A few Chinese parents I have met described cultural difficulties between their children and local European peers, and some transferred out. That does not describe every pupil or make ISL a poor school. It does mean integration should be discussed concretely.</p><p>When I raised Chinese pupils’ specific needs, ISL repeatedly emphasised equal treatment. In those conversations, including with senior staff, I did not receive the practical responses I was seeking. Based on my own interactions, St George’s was most responsive to these concerns, OTR next and ISL last. Families should understand this as my judgement from local work, rather than a general ranking of educational quality.</p><h2>Where LEP helps</h2><p>We connect curriculum choices, the family’s budget, school discussions and local living arrangements. English conversation alone does not establish readiness for academic reading and writing; older applicants need careful curriculum matching. ISL is a day school, so a child arriving without parents also needs a suitable local home. LEP offers host-family matching and practical coordination.
