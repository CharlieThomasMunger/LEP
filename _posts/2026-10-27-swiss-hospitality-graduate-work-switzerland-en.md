<h2>Citizenship changes the employment starting point</h2>
<p>During our Swiss hospitality-school visits, I specifically discussed employment for Chinese graduates. Schools described substantial international internship opportunities while acknowledging that moving directly into long-term Swiss employment is difficult for many new graduates.</p>
<div class="table-scroll"><table><thead><tr><th scope="col">Student status</th><th scope="col">Main distinction</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Swiss citizen</td><td>No foreign-worker admission requirement.</td></tr><tr><td>EU/EFTA citizen</td><td>Employment generally follows free-movement arrangements, rather than the same third-country admission system.</td></tr><tr><td>Other citizenship, including Chinese</td><td>Employer applications, quotas and job requirements generally matter. Eligible graduates of Swiss higher-education institutions may benefit from specific provisions.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>A residence card from an EU country is not EU citizenship and does not automatically confer a right to work in Switzerland. LEP starts with the student’s actual status when discussing a destination.</p>

<h2>An internship builds a career; permanent employment needs another decision</h2>
<p>I ask how internship support connects to the next job: who reviews applications, whether placements match the student’s interests, how performance is recognised and what support exists for applications in other countries. These details matter more than a headline employment rate.</p>
<p>IMI’s current hospitality-business bachelor includes at least 12 months of paid internships. Les Roches incorporates two internships, while Glion includes two internships and further professional experience. The precise programme, location and timing should be matched to the student’s goal.</p>
<p>Internship earnings belong in the budget after payroll deductions and placement living costs. See <a href="/en/insights/swiss-hospitality-bachelor-total-cost/">the full-degree cost and net internship-income comparison</a>.</p>

<h2>Check the qualification behind the graduate provisions</h2>
<p>Eligible Swiss higher-education graduates have a job-search arrangement; employment of high scientific or economic interest may also benefit from facilitated admission. The relevant qualification and job conditions matter. A school’s Swiss address alone does not establish eligibility.</p>
<p>Before a family commits, LEP can clarify the awarding body, the institution and programme status, and how graduates of that particular course approach employment formalities. A six-month job-search period is not an automatic work permit at its end.</p>

<h2>A Switzerland-only goal leads to a different choice</h2>
<p>If the family would accept only long-term work in Switzerland, I would first examine languages, professional strengths, realistic roles and status. Beautiful campuses and plentiful internships cannot settle that decision.</p>
<p>If the student is open to Europe, the Middle East or Asia, international employer networks become more relevant. Each destination has its own employment conditions. At Les Roches, our careers conversations included business and entrepreneurship; at IMI, academic learning and value stood out. The task is to connect those strengths to this student.</p>

Our third-party perspective comes from placing those accounts alongside one another. Upcoming visits will focus more on recruitment events, and we will update families with findings that can be checked.</p>
