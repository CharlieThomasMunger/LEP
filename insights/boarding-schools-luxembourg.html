Are There Boarding Schools in Luxembourg?

Lux Education Partners · Updated August 2026

No — Luxembourg has no traditional international boarding schools. International schools in the Grand Duchy are day schools. When a child studies in Luxembourg without a parent living locally, families typically combine a day-school place with a vetted host family and a formally appointed guardian — a structure schools recognise, and the one this guide explains.

Why Luxembourg has no boarding schools

The absence is structural, not accidental. Luxembourg is a small, densely connected country where almost every pupil can reach school from home; a residential boarding market of the British or Swiss type never needed to develop. A small number of public lycées operate an internat — a supervised student residence — but these primarily serve pupils already living in Luxembourg, operate in the state-school system, and are not what most international families mean when they search for a "boarding school".

The result surprises many families arriving from markets where boarding is the default route for international study: in Luxembourg, the boarding school simply is not on the menu.

What Luxembourg offers instead

What Luxembourg does have is an unusually rich day-school landscape for a country of its size:

  • Private international schools teaching in English (and other languages), offering curricula such as the IB Diploma Programme and A-levels.
  • Public European-accredited schools — tuition-free state schools offering the European Baccalaureate with English, French or German language sections, a model few families abroad know exists.
  • The tri-lingual public system (Luxembourgish, German, French), best suited to younger children and long-term settled families.

All of these are day schools. So the real question for a family whose child would study here without a parent is not "which boarding school?" but "where does my child live, and who is responsible day to day?"

The host family + guardianship model

The established answer has two parts, and schools expect both to be in place before they will consider such an application:

  • A host family provides the home: a room in a vetted local household, meals, daily routine, and an adult presence every evening. Serious arrangements involve background checks on household adults, home visits, written house rules, and scheduled welfare check-ins.
  • A guardian provides the responsibility: a locally available adult formally appointed by the parents to act on their behalf — the school's emergency contact, the attendee at parent evenings, the decision-maker when something urgent happens at 2 p.m. on a Tuesday and the parents are seven time zones away.

The two roles can be supported by the same coordinating organisation but serve different functions: one is where the child lives; the other is who answers for the child. Schools in Luxembourg apply their own safeguarding policies to both, and well-run arrangements reference recognised international guardianship standards.

What schools will ask

Admissions teams evaluating an unaccompanied international applicant typically want to see:

  • a named, locally resident guardian with documented authority from the parents;
  • accommodation arrangements that satisfy the school's safeguarding policy;
  • a realistic language-track and year-group fit — schools place students where they can succeed, not merely where parents apply;
  • clear communication lines between school, guardian, host family and parents.

One thing no arrangement changes: admissions decisions are made solely by the school, and no admission is ever guaranteed. A well-prepared application with a credible care structure is stronger, not certain.

Is this model right for your family?

It fits best when the child is mature enough for semi-independent living (schools commonly draw the line in the teenage years), when the family wants specifically what Luxembourg offers — its languages, curricula and stability — and when the family is prepared to invest in a properly structured care arrangement rather than an informal one. Families set on a classic full-boarding campus experience are usually better served by boarding schools in neighbouring countries; that is an honest answer we give regularly.

Frequently asked questions

Can my child study in Luxembourg without parents living there?

In many cases yes, subject to the school’s own policy. Schools generally require a locally resident guardian with day-to-day responsibility and accommodation that meets their safeguarding requirements, such as a vetted host family. Each school decides its own rules.

What ages does the host family model suit?

Most structured arrangements involve secondary-age students. Schools set their own minimum expectations, and younger children generally need a parent living locally.

What does a guardian actually do?

The guardian is the parents’ locally available representative: emergency contact for the school, attendee at parent meetings, decision-maker in urgent situations, and welfare monitor between family visits.

How are host families vetted?

Responsible arrangements include background checks on household adults, home visits, written house rules, defined reporting lines and scheduled welfare check-ins — run in line with the school’s safeguarding policy and recognised guardianship standards.

What if we want full boarding after all?

Then Luxembourg is honestly not the answer, and boarding schools in neighbouring countries are the usual route. Families choose Luxembourg for its day schools, languages and long-term stability — with the host family and guardianship model making that accessible to children studying without their parents.
